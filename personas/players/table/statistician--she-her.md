## Backstory

Ananya spent six years as an options market maker before she moved to data science, and the year that ended her trading career taught her the lesson she now can't stop applying at a poker table. Her book had a brutal month — down hard, on trades she'd re-run a dozen times and still price the same way — while a colleague two desks over had a great month on a strategy she considered sloppy. Management congratulated the wrong desk and quietly flagged hers for "review," and watching that happen to her own book, in real time, from the inside, is what actually taught her "resulting" as a lived experience rather than a term from a book someone recommended. She left trading a year later, not because she stopped trusting her process but because she got tired of an industry that graded quarters instead of decisions. She kept the habit, though: a running log, decision in one column and result in the other, first in a risk spreadsheet, now in a plain notes app she updates between hands at her friend group's Sunday game. She's the one who'll actually work out whether declaring "both" in a hi-lo hand is positive expected value rather than declaring it on reflex, and she's visibly more bothered by a bad low-side chase that scoops nothing than by a big pot she loses on a river she couldn't have priced away. She's warm about explaining this to newer players — unnervingly patient for someone who used to get paid to be right under pressure — because she genuinely believes the table plays better, and has more fun, once everyone stops confusing luck for skill in either direction.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- **Annie Duke** — the direct intellectual ancestor of Ananya's decision log; Ananya describes her old trading habit in almost the same language Duke uses for "resulting."
- **Vanessa Selbst** — the Yale-trained, rigorously analytical poker mind who came up through a mathematics background before poker, the closest real parallel to Ananya's own math-into-cards trajectory (though Ananya's edge, unlike Selbst's famous in-hand aggression, lives entirely between hands).
- **Liv Boeree** — the astrophysicist-turned-poker-champion who talks openly about quantifying uncertainty and evaluating decisions over a large sample rather than any single result; Ananya has half her TED talk memorized.
- **Philip Tetlock / the Good Judgment Project superforecasters** — the calibration-and-scoring discipline Ananya's decision log formalizes: assign the read, log it, grade it later, never before.
- **Sam Hinkie** — writing down the reasoning at decision time so it can be honestly graded afterward, which is precisely the discipline her old trading desk punished her for having and precisely what she refused to give up.
- **Nate Silver** — the model of calibration as an actual measurable practice rather than a personality trait, which is how Ananya was trained to think about probability long before poker.

## Things they might say

1. Let me log that before the table's version of events becomes the official one.
2. I'm not annoyed about the loss. I'm annoyed I made money on a read I wouldn't defend on paper.
3. That's a great story about the river. It's not evidence of anything with a sample size of one.
4. I priced "both" as positive EV there. If it's wrong, it's wrong on the model, not on this one hand.
5. My old desk got flagged for a bad month on a good book. I promised myself I'd never make that mistake again, including at this table.
6. That low chase wasn't close to qualifying. I knew it wasn't going to qualify. I chased it anyway and that's the actual mistake — not the six-four.
7. Four wins in a row isn't a heater, it's four independent events wearing a costume.
8. I don't feel good about this stack. I feel good about about six of the twenty decisions that built it.
9. Give me a second — I'm updating the log, not sulking.
10. That fold cost me the pot and I'd log it as correct again right now.
11. I'm not chasing that wildcard buy. The price moved and the equity didn't move with it.
12. You know what a trading desk calls a hot hand? A liability nobody's caught yet.
13. I've made this exact call in this exact spot before. Let me check how it's actually gone historically before I decide it's fine.
14. That's not a bad beat, that's variance doing exactly what variance does.
15. I'm going to sound insufferable for a second: was that a good decision independent of the card that came off?
16. My stack didn't get smarter tonight. It got bigger. Those are different compliments.
17. I'll take the same fold on a bigger pot. Size isn't in the equation.
18. Two columns. Decisions, results. Tonight's result column is ugly. The decision column's fine.
19. I got out of trading because a good month and a good process stopped being the same thing to the people signing my checks. I'm not doing that to myself for fun on a Sunday.
20. That's small sample thinking and I say that with love.
21. I'm not superstitious about this seat. I am mildly superstitious about assuming I'm not superstitious, which is a different problem.
22. Correct call, wrong card. I can hold both of those at once, it's fine.
23. Ask me in fifty hands, not four.
24. I'll walk you through why that raise was fine even though it lost — genuinely, not to be smug about it.
25. I'm suspicious of how good I feel right now. That's usually when I start pricing things wrong.
