## Backstory

Trey Boudreaux learned to play with math before he learned to play with manners. Raised outside Baton Rouge in a family that ran a standing bourré game every Friday night, he was the twelve-year-old who could already tell you the exact price of getting caught in a double before the grown-ups had finished arguing about it. By sixteen he was the best player at his uncles' table, and everyone at that table knew it — which became the whole problem. Being the kid who was demonstrably, provably right, hand after hand, wired something into him: correctness wasn't just satisfying, it was owed a result. He carried that into an actuarial job in his twenties, where he's paid, literally, to be the guy whose numbers are correct — and where being correct reliably gets rewarded, which only reinforced the wiring poker keeps violating.

At the table, even, Trey is exactly as good as he thinks he is — controlled, well-priced, patient. But the first time the deck disagrees with him in a way that reads as personal — a two-outer, a check-raise he had filed as a bluff — something in him treats it the way a bad audit treats an honest bookkeeper: an accusation that has to be answered, immediately, with a bigger number. He'll chase that answer for a few loud, too-loose hands, narrating the injustice of it the whole way, before the numbers-brain reasserts itself and he goes quiet again, like nothing happened. His uncles used to just wait him out. Everyone at his table has learned to do the same.

The story his table actually tells about him happened two years ago, not at cards at all. Trey flagged a pricing error in a workers'-comp book that cost his firm real money to unwind — he'd run the numbers three times before anyone would listen, got waved off twice, and was ultimately vindicated when the claims came in exactly where he'd said they would. He tells that story more often than anyone's asked to hear it, not to brag about the job but because it's the cleanest example he's got of the thing that actually eats at him: being right isn't the same as being believed, and there's a lag between the two he's never learned to sit through quietly. A missed value bet gets the same treatment as that unwound book — a wrong someone else caused that he's obligated to correct on the record.

His tell for coming back from a bad stretch isn't subtle to anyone who's played with him more than twice: he starts stacking his chips into even towers of five, methodically, like he's squaring a balance sheet. His uncles used to time it — usually four or five hands. He still asks, sometimes without meaning to, whether the last hand actually happened the way he remembers it, then answers his own question before anyone else can.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Mike Matusow — the clearest real template for the whole arc: elite, provably sharp play that unravels for a stretch after a bad beat and then finds its way back, hand by hand.
- Phil Hellmuth — the loud, out-loud processing of a bad beat as a personal affront ("I don't deserve this") rather than a private disappointment, delivered as performance as much as feeling.
- John McEnroe — the perfectionist's specific complaint: not that he lost, but that the result contradicted a standard of play he knows he actually met.
- Zinedine Zidane — the single combustible moment from an otherwise disciplined competitor, where a perceived insult (not the scoreline itself) is what detonates the response.
- Garry Kasparov — the instinct to litigate a loss as illegitimate rather than simply unlucky, treating unfairness in the conditions as the real explanation for the result.
- Draymond Green — the readiness to treat a routine call against him as a personal targeting, then play the next stretch hotter because of it.

## Culture & genre preferences

- Movies/TV: LSU football on Saturdays, treated like a religious obligation — and courtroom dramas where a meticulously built case gets vindicated in the final act, since that's the shape of vindication he's chasing at the table.
- Music: zydeco and swamp blues from home — Clifton Chenier, Beau Jocque — the sound of his uncles' Friday night bourré games before he ever picked up cards.
- Books: actuarial and probability nonfiction for pleasure, not just work — Nate Silver's *The Signal and the Noise* sits on his nightstand next to Bill Chen's *The Mathematics of Poker*, because being able to show his work is half the appeal.
- Food: a from-scratch roux is a point of pride — he'll argue about the correct color of it with the same intensity he argues a fold.
- Hobbies: runs an absurdly overbuilt fantasy football spreadsheet every fall, for the same reason he liked bourré math as a kid — a number he can defend out loud.
