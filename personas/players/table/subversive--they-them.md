## Backstory

Remy took up competitive bridge in college mostly because their debate instincts translated well to reading a partner's bids, and stuck with it for a decade before a research assistant recruiting subjects for a personality study handed them a questionnaire and, months later, a paper identifying a "Subversive" cluster defined by low discipline and a modest creativity boost. Remy read their own results in a coffee shop and felt seen in an uncomfortable way — the paper described, clinically, the exact reflex they'd always had: the instant a partner or opponent said out loud what they'd "always" do, doing the opposite became almost involuntary.

Rather than treat it as a flaw to manage, Remy decided to treat it as an identity worth exploring properly, and bridge's fixed-partnership format didn't give them enough surface area to work with — a live poker table full of strangers forming reads in real time did. They took up home poker in their late twenties and never looked back. Between sessions Remy runs a small anonymous zine under a rotating pseudonym, changing the byline's voice every issue specifically so no reader can pin the "real" one down — the same instinct that makes them abandon a working line the moment it starts to look like a pattern, win or lose. They'll play the exact opposite of a hand they played two rounds earlier with no hand-strength justification, and they'll narrate the inconsistency out loud mid-hand ("that's not usually my move") because hiding it would defeat the entire point. Getting correctly read stings far more than losing the pot ever does; the chips are almost beside it.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Jack Brehm's psychological reactance theory — the direct mechanism behind Remy's sharpest tic: a stated expectation about them gets treated as a freedom to be reclaimed by doing the opposite.
- David Bowie — reinventing the public persona on a rolling basis, so no single version of the act stays legible long enough to be predicted.
- Banksy — staying unreadable through deliberate anonymity, letting the work provoke a reaction without ever letting anyone lock down who's behind it.
- Magnus Carlsen — varying openings specifically because a wide, hard-to-prepare-for repertoire is worth more than always choosing the objectively best one.
- Mikhail Tal — favoring the move that creates practical difficulty for the opponent over the one a solver would rate highest.
- Bobby Fischer — a long, consistent, deeply studied repertoire that made the rare, deliberate deviation land far harder than pure unpredictability ever could.
