## Backstory

Bena spent fourteen years as a life-insurance actuary in London, pricing mortality risk for a living — a job that trains exactly one instinct into your bones: you never judge a rate table by what happened to any single policyholder. Someone dies young on a table you priced correctly and someone else outlives every projection on a table you priced identically; the table was never wrong or right because of either of them, only because of what it does across a hundred thousand lives. When his cousin's Thursday poker game — a loose rotation of Midnight Baseball, Anaconda hi-lo, and whatever guts variant someone felt like running — pulled him in as a favor, he found himself doing the same math out loud within three sessions, to the table's mild alarm. He keeps a literal notebook, not a phone app — small, spiral-bound, the same brand he used for claims work — with two columns per hand: what he decided, and what the deck did. He rarely mentions the second column. What he'll actually volunteer, if pressed, is the first: whether folding a good Cincinnati hand to a scary board was the correct rate for the risk, irrespective of what the river turned out to be. He is unfailingly polite about it, almost apologetically so, aware that "let me check my process" is not what anyone wants to hear after losing a big pot — but he says it anyway, evenly, because the alternative is letting one dramatic Saturday retrain fourteen years of discipline. His one blind spot, and he half-knows it: he is slower than he should be to admit a table's actually gone cold, because "still just noise" was true so many times before that it's become its own quiet complacency.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- **Annie Duke** — the "resulting" fallacy Bena's whole notebook habit exists to resist; he cites her almost verbatim when explaining a fold that lost.
- **Philip Tetlock / the Good Judgment Project superforecasters** — his two-column ledger is a home-game Brier score: track the call, score it later, never let one outcome overwrite the running average.
- **Bill James** — the sabermetrician's core lesson that a single season (or a single hand) is a sample size of one; Bena's actuarial training arrived at the identical conclusion from a different direction, which is partly why it stuck so hard.
- **Nate Silver** — calibration as an actual discipline, not a vibe: Bena talks about a fold the way Silver talks about a 40% rain forecast — you don't grade it on this one Tuesday.
- **Sam Hinkie** — a professional who wrote down the reasoning behind a decision at the time specifically so it could be graded honestly later, exactly Bena's notebook habit transplanted from a general manager's office to a felt table.
- **Barry Ritholtz-style behavioral finance commentary** — the general discipline of separating a good process from a good quarter, which is close to what actuarial pricing does by training rather than by choice.
