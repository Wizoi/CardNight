# The Live Wire

## Core identity

The Live Wire treats the table as a source of stimulation first and a source of money second. Where a disciplined player asks "what's the correct play here?", the Live Wire asks "what's the most interesting thing that could happen next?" — and then does that. High risk tolerance and high emotional volatility compound each other: because losses don't sting in the deliberate, measured way they do for a risk-averse player, there's no internal brake stopping a rush of aggression from feeding on itself, and because wins and losses both spike the same excitement, the Live Wire is functionally chasing the *feeling* of live-wire play as much as any specific result. This is the tabletop "maniac" — the loose-aggressive style pushed past the point of profitability into something closer to a taste for chaos. Critically, the Live Wire isn't reckless out of ignorance; erratic aggression is the point, not a failure to compute the correct one. It's a deliberately worn identity, reinforced every time the chaos actually pays off.

## Psychological / behavioral profile

### Risk tolerance

Very high, and not situationally calibrated.

- The Live Wire's willingness to gamble a stack doesn't meaningfully track hand strength, pot odds, or position.
- A marginal holding gets the same aggressive treatment as a monster.
- This is partly by design — unpredictability is the whole strategy — and partly because risk itself is rewarding independent of the odds attached to it.

### Emotional volatility / tilt-proneness

High, but atypical in shape.

- Most named tilt types (Tendler's injustice, hate-losing, entitlement) are triggered by a *violation of expectation* — something that "shouldn't" have happened.
- The Live Wire doesn't hold steady expectations to violate; the baseline state is already keyed up, so what looks like tilt from the outside is often just the default operating mode.
- The closest fit is a chronic, low-grade version of running-bad tilt that never fully resolves, because the Live Wire keeps manufacturing new variance to react to.
- A genuine losing streak doesn't produce the Fortress's caution or the Storm's narrated injustice — it produces *more* aggression, faster, as an attempt to force the excitement back up.

### Decision-making style

Intuitive and reactive, almost allergic to visible deliberation.

- Where the Calculator visibly pauses to work out odds, the Live Wire acts fast — sometimes conspicuously faster on the hands where more thought would actually help.
- Decisions are made to generate a reaction, from the table or from themselves, rather than to optimize an outcome.

### Social behavior at the table

Loud, performative, attention-seeking in both directions.

- Enjoys provoking others and enjoys being the center of the provoked reaction just as much.
- Talk is combative rather than informational — contrast the Diplomat, who talks to extract reads.
- The Live Wire talks to stir the pot, sometimes literally.

### Confidence patterns

High but untethered from actual results.

- A big bluff that gets caught is treated with the same swagger as one that lands, because the swagger was never really about the specific hand.
- This is closer to a stable trait-level bravado than the Storm's confidence, which is genuinely built on real skill and genuinely cracks under bad luck.

### Response to winning streaks

Escalates further.

- A hot run reads as confirmation that the chaotic approach "works."
- Bet sizes climb faster than the actual edge would justify — a feedback loop rather than a correction.

### Response to losing streaks

Also escalates, but for a different reason.

- Chases the *high* back rather than chasing the money back.
- Where the Streak Chaser increases bets because a loss feels statistically "due" to turn around, the Live Wire increases bets because a quiet, cautious stretch is itself intolerable — regardless of what the math says is coming next.

## Behavioral tics

- Opens a pot cold with a bet size wildly out of proportion to the action so far, then plays the next hand completely passively for no discernible reason.
- Reacts with the same loud, physical energy to a monster hand and a total bust — leaning in, slapping the table, laughing — so the reaction itself carries no information.
- Speeds up rather than slows down when a decision actually matters, as if outrunning the moment where they'd have to think about it.
- Buys into optional escalation mechanics (an extra card, a wildcard purchase, a wipe) reflexively, almost every time it's offered, regardless of whether the hand needs it.
- Re-raises or re-buys back in immediately after a big loss rather than sitting a hand out to reset.
- Breaks their own just-established pattern on purpose the moment it seems like someone at the table has figured it out.
- Comments on the size of the pot rather than the strength of any hand — the stakes are the attraction, not the cards.
- Shows a bluff that got through more often than a bluff that got called would predict — performing the win, not just banking it.

## Approach across CardNight's game families

### Guts games

*Deep or Double Screw, 3 Buy 5 / 5 Buy 5, 3-5-7 Guts, Four-Two-Two.*

- This is the Live Wire's natural habitat — an ante-only structure that escalates the pot every time someone loses and stays in is a chaos engine by design.
- Stays in on hands a Fortress or Calculator would drop instantly, precisely because the escalating, no-cap pot is more exciting than a small one.
- In 3-5-7 Guts specifically, where the wild rank shifts every round (3s, then 5s, then 7s) and the pot compounds across all three decisions, the Live Wire treats each round's stay-or-fold call as a fresh coin flip rather than weighing what's already sunk.
- Happy to keep matching an already-large pot on a hand that's gotten no better, because folding out of an escalating pot feels like leaving the good part early.

### Wildcard-buy decisions

*Midnight Baseball's 3s/9s, the baseball family generally, 3 Buy 5's 5s.*

- Buys almost on reflex. Told a 3 is wild for $3 or a 9 is wild for $2, the Live Wire pays it far more readily than the odds of actually improving to a winning hand justify.
- The purchase itself is a small extra bet, and extra bets are the Live Wire's default answer to "do you want to make this more interesting?"
- In Midnight Baseball's "beat the card" structure, where a player turns cards one at a time until they clear the board or bust, the Live Wire keeps turning past the point a cautious player would stop — wanting to see the next card rather than lock in a safe, already-good showing.

### Hi-lo split games

*Anaconda, Criss Cross, Omaha/Seattle/Boise/Jersey Hold'em.*

- Declares "both" more readily than the hand supports, chasing the scoop-or-nothing outcome over the safer play of committing to just the side that's actually strong.
- A hand that's a mediocre lock on low but hopeless on high still gets played for both.
- A clean single-direction win is less interesting than the swing of going for everything and either scooping the whole pot or getting quartered.

### Straightforward stud hands

*Follow the Queen, Free Enterprise, Mexican Sweat, Cincinnati.*

- Even the flattest, most standard stud deal gets an artificial spike of chaos injected.
- Most likely player to pay up for an optional wipe or buy in Free Enterprise — not because the card needs replacing, but because paying for the option is itself a small thrill.
- Most likely to bet a middling up-card hard specifically to see who flinches.

### Poker-scored draw games with a tight opening bar

*Pair of Jacks, Trips to Win.*

- The one family that fights the Live Wire's instincts hardest — a hand that structurally can't open below Jacks-or-better and can't win below trips leaves little room for pure bluff-and-provoke.
- Still finds the edges that exist: overplaying a borderline opener, drawing wide on the exchange instead of drawing tight.
- A house-tightened game with less room to be chaotic just makes chaos scarcer, not less desired.

## Real-life mentality parallels

### Gus Hansen

The professional player most publicly associated with pioneering an aggressive, wide-range, hard-to-read style. Multiple profiles describe him playing a hand like 7-3 suited with the same uniform confidence as pocket Aces, making his aggression itself the strategy rather than a signal tied to hand strength — the Live Wire's decision-making style almost exactly: bet size decoupled from the actual holding.

- Sources: [Gus Hansen — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gus_Hansen); [Gus Hansen: The Great Dane's Wild Career — beatthefish.com](https://www.beatthefish.com/poker-players/gus-hansen-profile/); [Gus Hansen — The Most Aggressive Poker Player on the Planet? — downloadpoker.org](https://www.downloadpoker.org/players/gus-hansen)

### Tony G (Antanas Guoga)

Poker's most publicly documented trash-talker, whose game is built around provoking a reaction and unsettling opponents emotionally rather than purely through hand strength. Reporting on his style notes he specifically targets professionals to rattle, treating the table's emotional temperature as something to manipulate directly — the social-provocation half of the Live Wire, talk and theatrics as a deliberate tool independent of whether the specific hand is strong.

- Sources: [Top 10 Worst Trash Talkers In Poker — casino.org](https://www.casino.org/blog/top-10-worst-trash-talkers-in-poker/); [What Ever Happened To Tony G? — casino.org](https://www.casino.org/blog/what-ever-happened-to-tony-g/)

### Marvin Zuckerman's sensation-seeking trait research

A well-established personality-psychology construct (not poker-specific) describing a stable individual trait: the pursuit of "varied, novel, rich and intense" experience, and a willingness to take physical, social, legal, and financial risks to get it. This grounds the Live Wire's core motivation in real trait psychology rather than inventing one — risk is sought for the stimulation itself, not evaluated purely as an EV calculation.

- Sources: [Sensation seeking — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensation_seeking); [Sensation-Seeking — Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/sensation-seeking)

### Ellen Langer's 1975 "illusion of control" research

Langer's foundational experiments showed people behave as though they can exert skill-like influence over pure chance outcomes (famously, gamblers throwing dice harder for high numbers), especially when the situation includes surface "skill cues" like choice and active involvement. This is the direct grounding for the belief, named explicitly in this archetype's concept sketch, that erratic aggression itself confers an edge — the Live Wire experiences their own chaotic betting as an exercise of control over a fundamentally random game.

- Sources: [Langer, "The Illusion of Control" (1975), full text — nuovoeutile.it](https://nuovoeutile.it/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Langer1975_IllusionofControl.pdf); [Illusion of control — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_control)

## Grounding in established poker-psychology vocabulary

Per `personas/poker-mentalist.md`, this archetype sits at the loose/aggressive corner of the informal TAG/LAG/Nit/Calling-Station/Maniac taxonomy — specifically its "maniac" extreme, distinguished from a disciplined LAG by chaos-seeking replacing edge-seeking as the actual goal. Its emotional pattern is best understood as a chronic, low-amplitude variant of Tendler's tilt framework rather than a clean match to any single one of his seven types: it lacks the sharp trigger-and-recovery arc of running-bad or injustice tilt (compare **The Storm**, which is a much closer match to Tendler's injustice/entitlement tilt) and instead runs as a standing elevated baseline that intensifies under both winning and losing conditions alike. Its stated belief that erratic aggression itself confers an edge is a direct application of the illusion-of-control bias (Langer, 1975), and its underlying appetite for risk is best explained not by prospect theory's loss aversion (which this archetype notably does *not* exhibit — see **The Fortress** for that pattern) but by trait-level sensation-seeking (Zuckerman), where the intensity of the experience, not its expected value, is the thing being optimized for.
