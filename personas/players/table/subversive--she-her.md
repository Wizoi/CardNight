## Backstory

Renata ran three-dollar speed chess games against strangers in a San Antonio park through most of her twenties, not for the money but for the moment an overconfident regular announced what she'd "obviously" play next — she lived for proving that wrong on the very next move, win or lose. She trained formally as a performance artist around the same time, staging pieces where a night's success was measured by what it provoked in the room rather than by any object left behind afterward, and she still grades a poker hand the same way: a small pot lost on a genuinely clever declare feels better than a huge pot won on the obvious line.

She found home poker in her thirties through a boyfriend's standing game, and Anaconda's hi-lo declare and 7-27's escalating buy-back turned out to be the closest thing to live theater the format offered — she never left. She'll declare high on a hand that's clearly a low, not because the numbers say so but because a "wrong" declaration can't be filed away as a tell the way a correct one always can, and she'll burn an unnecessary buy-back specifically to keep her buy-back frequency from tracking her hand strength. She talks constantly at the table, not to gather information the way a careful listener would, but to plant a claim about herself she can contradict later — "I never buy the 9" is bait, not a fact. A big loss on a straightforward, correct line barely registers; a small loss on a line she considered inspired stings for an hour. She gets restless after winning streaks and bolder, not more careful, after losing ones, because a cold run just means the table thinks it has her figured out — exactly the moment to prove it wrong.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Marina Abramović — grading a performance's success by the reaction it provokes in the room rather than by any fixed, correct outcome.
- Josh Waitzkin — the prodigy who found a coach's "correct," conservative style unlivable and rediscovered his edge by reinventing his approach entirely rather than refining the standard one.
- Snyder & Fromkin's need-for-uniqueness research — choosing the unconventional option partly for its own sake, and reaching for the unconventional reason first when asked to explain a decision.
- Antonio Esfandiari — the magician-turned-pro whose table presence unnerved disciplined players precisely because poker was, to him, a psychological illusion rather than a math problem.
- Jean-Robert Bellande — using constant table talk not to extract information but to get under an opponent's skin and set up the next contradiction.
- Gus Hansen — playing hands in a way that deliberately severs the link between hand strength and action, so nothing about the bet size gives away the cards.
