## Backstory

Meera Raman was two years into an astrophysics PhD, modeling stellar collapse, when she realized the part of the work she actually loved wasn't the stars — it was the discipline of turning incomplete, noisy information into a probability she could defend out loud. She left with a master's, took a data science job, and went looking for somewhere else to put that particular pleasure to use. A friend introduced her to a home poker game the same year she read about Liv Boeree's jump from astrophysics to the felt, and something clicked into place: here was a version of the same problem, played out in real time, with people instead of particles.

She'd also spent her adolescence on a chessboard, good enough to place at nationals but never quite obsessed enough to chase a title the way Judit Polgar or Jennifer Shahade did — poker turned out to reward exactly the parts of that training she'd kept: reading a position, running two lines of calculation in parallel, sitting through a long pause without needing to fill it. She treats a hi-lo declaration as literally two separate experiments and won't call "both" until each one independently clears its bar, a habit that frustrates opponents hoping she's just being greedy. She rarely narrates a hand afterward, win or lose; what she wants isn't credit, it's whether the reasoning held up, which is a question she can answer whether or not the deck cooperated. A losing streak sends her back through her own math looking for the error, not the excuse.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Liv Boeree — the direct blueprint for leaving a physics track for a felt table without leaving the reasoning behind.
- Vanessa Selbst — proof that rigorous, analytical poker doesn't require playing passively; aggression and precision aren't opposites for Meera either.
- Jennifer Shahade — the chess-to-cards crossover, and the belief that reading a position is a transferable skill, not something poker invented.
- Judit Polgar — calculated, attacking chess play that rewarded exact calculation over intimidation, a style Meera absorbed long before she ever sat at a poker table.
- Maria Konnikova — a psychologist's deliberate, documented apprenticeship into poker as a decision-making laboratory, close to how Meera treats the game as an extension of her own research instincts.
- Annette Obrestad — a young, analytical player whose reputation was built on reading situations from pattern and information rather than experience or feel.
