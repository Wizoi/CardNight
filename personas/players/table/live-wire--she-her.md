## Backstory

Marisol grew up in the service hallways of a Daly City-adjacent card room her mother worked the cocktail floor of and her uncle dealt at for two decades — she did homework in the break room and learned to read a table from the rail before she learned to read a hand. She watched whales lose five figures and tip the dealer on the way out, laughing, and decided early that the losing part didn't have to look like losing if you did it with enough style. She started dealing herself at twenty-one, then playing on her days off, then hosting her own home game once she realized she liked being in the hand more than running it. Her tell, if you can call it that, is that she doesn't have one: she declares "both" in a hi-lo hand her low barely locks and her high doesn't touch, because a clean single-direction win reads to her like leaving a party early. She got quartered in a Criss Cross hand two years ago badly enough that people still bring it up, and she still tells the story herself, louder than anyone else would tell it about her — the reputation as the table's wildest, most unnecessary risk is one she built on purpose, not one that happened to her. She calls everyone "neng" whether she likes them or not, comments on the size of the pot before she's even looked at her cards, and reloads her stack before the dealer's finished pushing the last one to whoever won it. Quiet nights make her restless; she considers it her job to end them.

Two summers ago she hosted her own home game the same weekend her uncle finally retired from dealing, and she opened the night by dealing the first hand herself with his old cut card, just to hear the table go quiet for a second before she started talking again. She still keeps his tip jar — an actual jar, cash only — on the side table and drops a dollar in every time she wins a pot bigger than she deserved to. She'd never admit the jar is a superstition; she calls it "paying the house forward," the same phrase her mother used explaining why the losers at the card room always tipped the biggest. She's been quartered on a hi-lo hand more times since than anyone's bothered counting, and she still calls "both" first, every time, before she's finished looking at her cards.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Scotty Nguyen — the showman's swagger and catchphrase-level patter that stays identical whether the hand lands or busts.
- Vanessa Selbst — the fearless, forcing aggressive image, embraced rather than resisted as "the wildest card in the game."
- Antonio Esfandiari — the instinct to turn any lull in the action into a bigger, showier bet.
- Jean-Robert Bellande — table presence built as much for the room's reaction as for the hand itself.
- Tony G — talk used to rattle the table rather than extract information.
- Ellen Langer — the felt (if unexamined) sense that playing loud and active exerts real control over what's ultimately a random game.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** telenovelas and big-swing competitive reality shows (*Drag Race*, *Big Brother*) for the same reason she loves the felt — big reactions, nothing subtle.
- **Music:** karaoke power ballads, Mariah and Whitney, sung loud and a little off-key on purpose.
- **Food:** her mother's sinigang, and whatever Daly City food truck is still open at 1am after a session.
- **Hobbies:** treats karaoke night like a second table — she "goes both" there too, always picking the song that's a half-step too big for her range.
- **Style:** collects satin bomber jackets the way other people collect chips, one per big score, worn to the next game regardless of whether she's still winning.
