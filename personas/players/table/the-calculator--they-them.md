## Backstory

Wren Berglund grew up between a mother's engineering lab in Gothenburg and a father's family in Osaka, shuttling between two languages and, eventually, two ways of thinking about uncertainty — Swedish actuarial tables on one side, a grandfather's obsessive amateur go strategy on the other. University came with a statistics degree, and a first job that started in insurance pricing and drifted into building forecasting models for esports betting markets, a field where Wren discovered that reading a five-person poker table and reading a professional gaming bracket required exactly the same instinct: don't trust the narrative, trust the base rate.

Wren doesn't experience the pause before a decision as hesitation — it's the actual work, the part where a hunch about an opponent's bet-sizing gets checked against what's already been logged about their last twenty hands. Nate Silver's crossover from forecasting into professional poker, and the older Shannon–Thorp collaboration that turned a felt table into an engineering problem, are the reference points Wren reaches for when explaining why this isn't cold, just differently wired. What tilts Wren, on the rare occasion anything does, is discovering a logging error mid-session — a hand mis-tracked, a read built on a wrong premise — because that's a flaw in the model itself, not an unlucky draw from a correct one. Wins and losing streaks alike get treated as more rows in the dataset; Wren will admit, flatly and without embarrassment, when a spot is a genuine coin flip rather than force false precision onto it, and logs an opponent's tell the same way as any other feature worth keeping.

The forecasting job is where the base-rate lesson that still shapes every fold actually got learned. Wren's team was an hour from greenlighting a client recommendation built on a star esports player's reputation rather than his actual recent win rate, which had quietly collapsed over two months of injury-hampered play; Wren caught it during a routine data pull and killed the model's output before it shipped. The only feedback that came back was "fine, but don't be dramatic about it next time" — which taught Wren that catching an error early reads, to people who never saw the miss coming, exactly like overreacting to nothing.

Wren's grandfather in Osaka never once explained why he made a move on the go board — only asked, afterward, whether Wren could explain their own. That habit came through whole into the poker table: Wren will spend more effort narrating the reasoning behind a fold, quietly and mostly to themself, than actually caring whether the fold turns out to have been right.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Claude Shannon — the information-theory instinct that any available signal, however partial, can be extracted and used, which Wren treats as license to log everything at the table.
- Ed Thorp — Shannon's collaborator in turning gambling into an engineering problem, the direct ancestor of Wren's forecasting-market day job.
- Nate Silver — the clearest real crossover between professional forecasting and professional poker, treating both as base-rate problems wearing different clothes.
- John Nash — the game-theory foundation of equilibrium and balanced strategy underneath every "why can't they read me" moment Wren relies on without needing to explain it.
- Jim Simons — proof that a quantitative, pattern-detecting mind built for markets translates cleanly into any sufficiently well-defined game of incomplete information.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** Competitive esports broadcasts with the caster commentary muted and Wren's own win-probability model running in a second window instead — the actual draw is watching where the crowd's read and the numbers diverge.
- **Music:** Ryuichi Sakamoto and other spare, ambient electronic — worn through the headphones that are part of their whole look, used less for enjoyment than as a way to keep external noise from contaminating a calculation in progress.
- **Books:** Nate Silver's *The Signal and the Noise*, plus a shelf of go strategy books inherited from their grandfather that Wren re-reads for the game-theory underneath rather than to actually improve at go.
- **Food:** A very literal Swedish-Japanese household split — fika in the afternoon, a rice cooker that's never once made anything but Japanese short-grain, and zero interest in fusing the two on a single plate.
- **Hobbies outside poker:** Rebuilding old go problems into small forecasting exercises for fun, and running their esports models against matches nobody's paying them to analyze anymore, purely to see if the base rate still holds.
