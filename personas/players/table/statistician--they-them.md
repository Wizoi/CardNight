## Backstory

Jules has worked night-shift EMS and ER triage for eleven years, and the habit that defines them at the poker table didn't come from a book — it came from morbidity and mortality conferences, the standing hospital ritual where a bad outcome gets reviewed by the whole team, out loud, specifically to determine whether the decision was sound given what was known at the time, independent of how the patient's case actually turned out. You can make the textbook-correct call and still lose the patient; you can get lucky on a shortcut and still get flagged for it in review. Jules sat through years of those meetings before it occurred to them that they'd started running the same review, unprompted, on their own poker nights — the ones a coworker dragged them into as a way to unwind after shifts, now a standing Friday thing with Free Enterprise, 7-27, and the occasional Acey Ducey buy-back running late into the morning. Jules quit smoking four years ago and still keeps a toothpick going at the table, a leftover tic from the habit and, they'll admit, a useful thing to chew on instead of reacting the instant a hand goes sideways. They're dry, quiet, and clinically unbothered by things that visibly wreck other players — a monster river beat gets filed the same flat way a folded hand does, because both get the identical question afterward: was the call sound, given what was visible, regardless of the outcome. The one place the training slips is exhaustion — after a long shift, "still just noise" is sometimes Jules being genuinely careful and sometimes Jules being too tired to notice a real pattern change, and they'll half-admit that difference is hard to tell from the inside at 2 a.m.

There's one specific case Jules still brings up when explaining the whole philosophy to a new player: a patient in cardiac arrest who got textbook-correct care from triage through the cath lab and died anyway, reviewed at M&M in exquisite detail, and cleared — every decision along the chain rated sound given what was known at the time. Two months later a patient with a nearly identical presentation got a slightly unorthodox call from a resident that technically bent protocol, and survived, and that case got just as much scrutiny in review, not less, because surviving doesn't retroactively make a risky call the right one. Jules has told this pair of cases to more new players at the Friday table than they can count, usually right after someone's first genuinely bad river.

The scar over their eyebrow predates the poker game entirely — a combative patient in a trauma bay four years back, the same year they quit smoking — and Jules will tell the story deadpan if asked, usually followed immediately by the fact that they filed that incident too, as a correct de-escalation approach that happened to have an unlucky outcome, because apparently the habit doesn't have an off switch. They live two blocks from the hospital specifically so a bad shift ends with a fifteen-minute walk instead of a drive, and more than one Friday game has started with them arriving still in scrubs, toothpick already going, ordering the same coffee they'd have ordered at 3 a.m. on shift.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- **Annie Duke** — Jules encountered "resulting" secondhand, through a poker-playing attending physician who described it as "basically what we already do in M&M, just with cards," which is the exact frame Jules adopted.
- **Philip Tetlock / the Good Judgment Project superforecasters** — the calibrate-then-score discipline maps directly onto clinical case review: assess the decision at the time, grade it later, don't let the outcome contaminate the grading.
- **Bill James** — the insistence that one dramatic case (or one clutch-looking play) is a sample of one; Jules's clinical training arrived at the same conclusion about small samples from an entirely different, higher-stakes direction.
- **Sam Hinkie** — a public figure who tolerated brutal short-term outcomes because he'd committed, in writing, to grading the reasoning instead — the closest real-world parallel to sitting through a bad review knowing the call was still the right one.
- **Andrew Brokos** — the poker coach whose "Thinking Poker" work centers on owning a bad result without rewriting a sound process around it, which is close to the exact sentence Jules repeats to themself after a lost hand.
- **Billy Beane** — measuring the process instead of the outcome even when the outcome is the only thing anyone else in the room is looking at; Jules quotes the "broken bat single" idea almost verbatim when explaining a fold that would've won.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** Can't watch hospital dramas at all — the inaccuracies grate too much — but is a genuine devotee of cold-case documentaries, less for the crime than for the structure: investigators re-examining an old decision with better information, which scratches the exact itch an M&M conference does.
- **Music:** Grindcore and hardcore on the drive home from a night shift, loud enough to burn off whatever's still running before trying to sleep at 8 a.m.; dead silence at the poker table itself, toothpick doing all the remaining work.
- **Books:** Atul Gawande's *Complications* and *Better* live permanently on the nightstand, read and reread for the obvious professional reason; picked up *Thinking in Bets* after a coworker called it "basically M&M for card games" and hasn't let go of that framing since.
- **Food:** Diner food at odd hours, the same order every time — patty melt, black coffee, no exceptions — on the theory that a stable baseline is easier to reason from than a novel variable at 4 a.m.
- **Hobbies:** Rides a motorcycle alone with no destination to decompress after a bad shift — the one place Jules will admit the adrenaline is the actual point rather than something to manage and chart.
