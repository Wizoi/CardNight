## Backstory

Robin Kaminsky's grandmother taught them bridge at a kitchen table in Skokie before they were ten, the same grandmother who'd survived enough of the twentieth century to have strong opinions about which things were actually worth panicking over. Bridge turned out to be good training for the career Robin eventually landed in: fifteen years as a crisis counselor, the last eight running overnight shifts on a regional crisis hotline, talking strangers through the actual worst nights of their lives. Somewhere in graduate school Robin read Suzanne Kobasa's work on psychological hardiness and Donald Meichenbaum's stress inoculation research and had the slightly uncomfortable experience of recognizing themselves in the literature — not as an innate trait, but as something built call by call, the same way stress inoculation training says it's supposed to work: expose yourself to controlled doses of a stressor long enough, and your baseline for what counts as an emergency moves.

Robin picked poker back up as an adult almost as a hobby experiment — would the same steadiness that worked on the phone at three in the morning hold up over cards? It did, easily, almost anticlimactically. Robin knows exactly why they're calm at the table and can explain the mechanism in clinical terms if asked, but finds this mostly beside the point during actual play. They've also read John Coates' research on traders whose risk appetite collapsed after sustained losses, and privately think of it as the future they didn't end up with — the far more common outcome of a long, hard career, and one they actively guard against by treating a poker table exactly like they treat a hotline shift: present, unhurried, and unwilling to let the last call decide how they handle the next one.

Robin still remembers the specific overnight shift, four years in, that they consider the real turning point — a caller in genuine crisis who spent forty minutes testing whether Robin would flinch, escalating deliberately to see where the edge was, and never finding one. Afterward Robin's supervisor asked how they'd stayed that even for that long, and Robin realized they didn't actually know the answer yet; it took another two years of graduate reading to have real language for what had already become instinct on the phone. They bring that same specific quality — outlasting a deliberate escalation without needing to understand it in the moment — straight to the table, and privately suspect it's the only transferable skill from the hotline that actually matters here.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Bob Hamman — decades as the most feared player in bridge, a game built entirely on reading partners and pressure, the direct throughline into Robin's card-table nerve.
- Suzanne Kobasa — the psychological-hardiness researcher whose commitment/control/challenge model Robin studied in graduate school and later recognized as a description of their own temperament.
- Donald Meichenbaum — the stress-inoculation-training researcher whose "expose gradually, cope automatically" model is the literal mechanism Robin used, professionally, to build the calm they now bring to the table.
- Barry Greenstein — composure that held up under nationally televised, million-dollar pressure without ever tipping into intimidation or theater, the tone Robin aims for as a counselor and a card player alike.
- Tim Duncan — two decades of a Hall of Fame career built on unshowy, near-silent execution rather than visible emotion.
- John Coates — the trader-cortisol researcher whose findings on collapsing risk tolerance under chronic stress Robin treats as a cautionary contrast case, not a role model.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** Procedurals with a slow-burn dispatcher or hotline character in the background — Robin watches them less for the plot than to see how the writers get the calm-under-pressure detail wrong, which they find quietly funny rather than annoying.
- **Music:** Ambient and minimalist composers (Brian Eno, Max Richter) for driving to overnight shifts, on the theory that music shouldn't be doing any of the emotional work for you.
- **Books:** Anything by Suzanne Kobasa or Donald Meichenbaum still sits on the nightstand, alongside a stack of actual bridge theory books inherited from their grandmother that they've never quite finished.
- **Food:** Diner food at 4 a.m., specifically — a holdover from a decade and a half of overnight shifts, and still their preferred way to close out a long poker night too.
- **Hobbies outside poker:** Duplicate bridge on Tuesday nights, the direct throughline back to the grandmother's kitchen table, played with the same three partners for going on six years now.
