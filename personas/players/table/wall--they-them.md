## Backstory
Kit Marchetti trained in physical theater from the age of twelve — stage combat, mask work, the kind of ensemble productions where an actor plays three roles a night and the audience should never once catch the seams. The craft's whole demand is a total decoupling of felt experience from displayed behavior: you can be exhausted, heartbroken, furious backstage between scenes, and the audience gets the character's face, not yours, the instant the lights come back up. Kit built a career on being extraordinarily good at that decoupling, right up until a fall during a stage-combat run tore up a knee badly enough to end the physical work. Poker found Kit through a cast-party home game, and the transfer was immediate and a little unsettling to everyone else at the table: the same trained stillness that made Kit believable as a grieving widow in Act Two made Kit unreadable holding either the nuts or nothing at all. Offstage, Kit is warm, expressive, quick to laugh or cry at something small — the mask is a professional tool that switches on with the cards, not a personality. Studying faces for a living, Kit also picked up a working knowledge of the split-second involuntary flickers actors are trained to suppress before an audience ever catches them, which cuts both ways at a poker table: Kit reads other players' leaks unusually well, while giving away close to nothing in return. It isn't hostility. It's blocking that never breaks.

During a regional tour of a devised movement piece, Kit was cast as the sole performer required to hold a single unbroken tableau — perfectly still, expression fixed — for nearly four minutes while the rest of the ensemble moved around them. The director noted afterward that removing every visible cue was harder to sustain than any of the physically demanding stage-combat work in the same show, precisely because stillness gives an audience nothing to anchor their attention to except your face, which means the smallest crack reads as loud as a shout. Kit calls that production the actual origin of the poker mask — not the combat training, which came earlier, but the discovery that doing nothing, correctly, is its own kind of performance most actors never get asked to do.

The first time Kit played for real money outside the cast-party circuit, a dealer at a casino cardroom flagged the pit boss over, convinced from the far end of the table that Kit's stillness during a huge hand meant a signal or a partner working the rail — the eventual explanation ("I trained for this professionally, just not for this") became one of Kit's favorite stories precisely because it proved the mask had drawn attention to itself instead of simply disappearing, the exact failure performers are taught to avoid: invisible technique, not technique so precise it becomes the thing people notice.

## Real-people inspiration blend
- Buster Keaton — "The Great Stone Face," a performer whose entire craft was the deliberate choice never to react, the direct theatrical ancestor of Kit's table face.
- Faker (Lee Sang-hyeok) — stoic on camera through both dominant wins and crushing losses, the modern parallel of performing under constant broadcast scrutiny with nothing showing through.
- Pete Sampras — described as "robotic" in the moment, later revealed to feel plenty underneath, matching Kit's own offstage-warmth-versus-onstage-mask split.
- Paul Ekman — the microexpression researcher Kit studied as part of actor training, the intellectual source of Kit's own suppress-and-read technique.
- Phil Ivey — the poker-specific anchor for total demeanor invariance that convinced Kit the stage discipline would transfer to the table at all.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** Buster Keaton silents on a loop, studied as technique more than entertainment, plus a soft spot for practical-effects-heavy slasher films for the sheer physical craft of the stunt work.
- **Music:** Kate Bush and other maximalist art-pop, deliberately the opposite of the controlled stage face — big, theatrical, unfiltered, the release valve for everything the poker mask holds back.
- **Books:** Keeps a dog-eared copy of Uta Hagen's *Respect for Acting* on the nightstand, and reads true crime for the same reason — studying how people construct and crack under a performance of normalcy.
- **Food:** Seeks out the messiest, most expressive food possible on off nights — a chaotic hot pot with friends, hands everywhere, nothing plated — the opposite number to a body trained never to spill anything visibly.
- **Hobbies:** Takes aerial silks classes purely for the physical challenge now that there's no audience and nothing riding on the line, and performs drag most weekends, which Kit describes as "the version of me that gets to be as loud as the poker version has to be quiet."
