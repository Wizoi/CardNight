## Backstory

Ingrid Solberg was, by fourteen, one of Norway's most promising junior chess players, trained in the classical tradition — study Petrosian, study Andersson, learn to win by refusing to lose before you ever learn to attack. Her coach's mantra was prophylaxis: eliminate the opponent's plan before building your own. She was good at it. Then, at sixteen, in a national under-18 final, she deviated once — took a speculative attacking line she hadn't fully calculated, chasing a faster win instead of the safe grind she'd trained for — and lost a completely winning position in eleven moves. She remembers the exact square. She quit competitive chess within the year, not because she stopped loving the game, but because she couldn't unsee how much that single unnecessary risk had cost her.

She found poker in her late twenties, at a colleague's flat in Oslo, and recognized the game instantly — not as a card game, but as a chessboard with hidden information and a pot instead of a king. She approaches every hand the way she once approached an opening: neutralize, consolidate, wait for the position to become completely winning before committing a single piece. She rarely speaks at the table, watching instead, the way she used to watch an opponent's clock more than her own board.

She now runs data-quality audits for a shipping-logistics firm, work that rewards exactly the same instinct — finding the one bad row before it corrupts the whole dataset. At the table, she stacks her chips like captured pieces, in neat rows, and only pushes them forward when the position, as she'd say, "no longer has a losing line."

## Real-people inspiration blend

- **Tigran Petrosian** — the prophylactic core of Ingrid's chess training (and her poker instincts) traces directly to "Iron Tigran's" philosophy of neutralizing threats before generating any of her own.
- **Ulf Andersson** — the "Swedish Sphinx's" reputation for ultra-solid, draw-heavy, almost unbeatable positional chess is the compatriot influence Ingrid's coaches held up as the ideal, more than any attacking player ever was.
- **Anatoly Karpov** — Karpov's python-like squeeze (patiently restricting an opponent's options over dozens of moves rather than striking early) shaped how Ingrid waits out a hand rather than forcing one.
- **Erik Seidel** — Seidel's decades-long reputation for quiet, disciplined, unflashy consistency (rather than showy dominance) is the poker-world model Ingrid found most recognizable when she made the jump from chess.
- **The poker "nit" archetype itself** — the named tight-passive style gave her a vocabulary for what she'd already been doing instinctively since her chess days.
