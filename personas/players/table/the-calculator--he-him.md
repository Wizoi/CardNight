## Backstory

Femi Adeyemi grew up between Lagos, where his father priced marine cargo insurance out of a cramped office near the port, and a boarding school outside London that his family scraped to afford. He learned two things early and never fully separated them: that a number can tell you the truth about a risk before anything bad has happened, and that a chessboard rewards the player willing to sit still and calculate rather than the one who moves first. He kept both — went into actuarial science at university, and kept a chess club membership going for fifteen years afterward, quietly admiring the patient, restrictive style of players like Karpov over the flashier attacking games everyone else watched.

By the time poker entered his life — a colleague's leaving-do game that turned into a standing Thursday habit — the transfer was almost frictionless. Pot odds are a loss ratio with a shorter horizon. A bad beat is a claim that happened to pay out; the underwriting was still sound. What did rattle him, in his first year of serious play, was a hand where he simply miscounted his outs and made a bet the math never supported — and he found that error sat with him for days in a way no lost pot ever had. That became the diagnostic he still uses: not did I win, but did I do the arithmetic correctly. Twenty years of pricing risk for a living made the pause before he acts feel less like deliberation and more like professional habit — the part of the job that was never optional.

Femi still keeps the actual spreadsheet — a session log going back to his first year of serious play, one row per sitting, buy-in, cash-out, hours played, and a single line flagging the one decision worth reviewing. He fills it in before he showers, not before he sleeps, because he learned early that fatigue makes a retrospective a moment too generous to itself. The Thursday game outlived the colleague who started it: when that friend moved back to Lagos for a parent's illness, the other five players quietly assumed the game would fold along with him, and Femi was the one who took over the group chat and the buy-in ledger — less out of sentiment than a flat refusal to let a good process die of neglect.

The story he actually reaches for when explaining why he trusts a fold that loses is a hull-and-cargo policy he killed early in his career, over a two-percent probability gap a junior colleague thought wasn't worth losing the account over. Everyone else at the underwriting table wanted the premium. The ship turned out fine. He still tells poker players it doesn't matter — the policy was wrong to write the moment they wrote it, and the safe voyage afterward didn't retroactively make the number good.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Bill Chen — reduces hand strength to explicit, repeatable arithmetic (the Chen Formula), the same instinct Femi brings from two decades of pricing risk professionally.
- Ed Thorp — proved an edge could be found through pure calculation and carried that discipline from blackjack into a second career; the same throughline runs from Femi's actuarial desk into his Thursday poker game.
- Chris Ferguson — the deliberately flat, motionless table demeanor adopted specifically to leak nothing, mirrored in Femi's unreadable insurance-broker poker face.
- Anatoly Karpov — the patient, restrictive, edge-accumulating chess style Femi has admired since his school chess club days, in explicit contrast to flashier attacking play.
- David Sklansky — the formal expected-value framework Femi had effectively been applying to claims files for years before he ever read it described in a poker book.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** *Enron: The Smartest Guys in the Room* on a loop — he treats it less as scandal than as a case study in what happens when nobody's willing to be the one who kills a bad number. Also partial to *Bobby Fischer Against the World* for the chess pedigree, though he finds Fischer's actual temperament borderline unwatchable.
- **Books:** Nassim Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* and Peter Bernstein's *Against the Gods* sit permanently on his nightstand — both are, in his words, "the same book his job already made him live."
- **Music:** Bill Evans and other unhurried, harmonically patient jazz piano — the kind of music that rewards not rushing the resolution, which he'll admit is exactly the appeal.
- **Food:** Jollof rice cooked to a Lagos-house recipe he's timed and weighed until it's reproducible to the minute, alongside a standing Sunday habit of pulling espresso by gram rather than eyeballing it.
- **Hobbies outside poker:** Still plays in his old chess club's weekend league, rated solidly mid-table and entirely at peace with never being the strongest player in the room, only the most consistent.
