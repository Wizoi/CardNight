## Backstory

Marcus grew up in Houston, the second of four kids in a family where every uncle seemed to be a tradesman of some kind — his father ran residential wiring jobs, and by seventeen Marcus was climbing attics with him, learning that live current doesn't reward hesitation; you commit to the cut or you don't touch the wire at all. He apprenticed, got licensed, and built a reputation as the guy other electricians called when a job needed someone who wouldn't flinch at a hot panel. The problem was that the feeling never left him when the truck pulled out of the driveway. Ordinary evenings felt underpowered. He found the fix at a coworker's Friday dealer's-choice game — a $2-ante Deep or Double Screw hand where the pot doubled twice before anyone folded gave him the exact same drop-in-the-stomach jolt as a panel he probably shouldn't have opened live. He kept coming back for that feeling, not the money, and it showed: he'd cold-raise a hand that didn't deserve it, then fold the next one flat, because the point was never the cards, it was the current running through the room. His regular game nicknamed him "Sparks" after he described losing a monster pot as "tripping the breaker" and grinning the whole time he racked down. He's rebought into more games in one sitting than anyone else at the table has bought in total, and he means it as a compliment to himself. He doesn't hide the tell that he has no tells — same wide grin whether he's stacking chips or reaching for his wallet again.

Marcus still tells the story of the Thanksgiving job that almost undid him: a customer's disconnect box read dead on the tester but wasn't, because a neighbor's illegally-tied generator was feeding it backward through the meter. He caught it by feel a half-second before contact — the hair on his arm, not the meter — and has treated that half-second ever since as proof that instinct beats measurement when it actually counts. He doesn't tell the story as a near-miss scare, though everyone else who's heard it treats it that way; he tells it as one of the best afternoons he's had in years. His home game has a standing rule, unofficial but universal, that if Marcus asks "you sure about that fold?" the answer is never yes.

## Real-people inspiration blend

- Gus Hansen — same bet size regardless of hand strength, aggression itself as the strategy rather than a signal.
- Mike Matusow — the loud, self-sabotaging "blowup" energy, and the candor that the volatility runs hotter than any specific hand justifies.
- Amarillo Slim — the hustler's reflex to make any lull "more interesting" by proposing action, needing the bet more than the odds.
- Tony G — table talk built to needle and unsettle rather than inform.
- John Daly — a non-poker gambler on record that the adrenaline, not the money, was always the actual draw.
- David "Devilfish" Ulliott — brash, combative table persona that treats provocation as half the game.

## Culture & genre preferences

- **Movies/TV:** *Days of Thunder* and the *Fast & Furious* franchise on constant rotation — cars, crowds, no scene that outstays its welcome.
- **Music:** ZZ Top and Southern rock played loud enough to drown out a job site, because a quiet truck cab feels a lot like a quiet table.
- **Food:** gas-station kolaches on the drive in, his mother's oxtail on Sundays — nothing plated, everything eaten standing up.
- **Hobbies:** drag racing a beat-up Nova at a bracket track outside Houston every other Saturday — same reasoning as the poker table, quarter-mile, all-in, no do-overs.
- **Books:** doesn't read much for pleasure, but has every NEC code revision basically memorized, which he insists is a completely different kind of reading.
